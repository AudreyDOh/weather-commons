Operation Popeye, Laos 1966–1972

For six rainy seasons the US military seeded clouds over Laos, trying to stretch the monsoon and turn the roads south into mud. What did it try to do to the weather, where did that weather go, and who lived under it?

1. Event

In October 1966 the US Department of Defense ran more than 50 cloud-seeding experiments over southern Laos. A State Department memo of January 1967 reports them and asks whether to make rain a weapon. Its aim was to slow the trucks carrying supplies south through Laos to the war in Vietnam.1 Later accounts say the seeding ran every rainy season until 1972.2

October 1966
DocumentedTests in "the valley of the Se Kong River", east of the Bolovens Plateau, "without consultation with Lao authorities". The Defense Department called the results "outstandingly successful": by its own count, 82% of the seeded clouds produced rain soon after.1
13 January 1967
DocumentedFoy Kohler writes to Secretary of State Dean Rusk. He proposes seeding along the supply routes in North Vietnam and southern Laos, "to inhibit overland vehicular movement" and, in Laos, "to extend rainfall through the dry season". He warns that the amount and spread of the extra rain cannot be predicted, and he writes on the memo, "I have serious doubts."1
1967–1972
ReportedSeeding with silver and lead iodide every rainy season, flown from Udon Thani in Thailand. The aims: soften roads, set off landslides, wash out river crossings.2
1971–1974
ReportedThe programme becomes public: a column by Jack Anderson (March 1971), a New York Times report by Seymour Hersh (3 July 1972)3 and a US Senate hearing on weather modification (20 March 1974).2
1976–1978
DocumentedThe UN General Assembly adopts ENMOD, the convention banning the military or hostile use of environmental modification (10 December 1976). It opens for signature in Geneva on 18 May 1977 and comes into force on 5 October 1978.4

The weather it worked on was the end of the monsoon. ERA5 rainfall at the Se Kong valley falls from about 216 mm in September 1966 to 120 mm in October and 53 mm in November. Popeye was meant to stretch that season.

2. Simulate

We can't model cloud seeding: whether it added any rain is exactly what nobody could measure. What we can show is the weather it worked on. That week, how did the air over the test area move, and how far, across which borders? The dots below start in the Se Kong valley and drift with the archived hourly wind for the first week of October 1966. Click the map to release them somewhere else.

CalculatedHow it works: each dot moves with the wind at its position every five minutes, with a little random spread for turbulence. The wind is read from the hourly ERA5 grid, 1° apart, 100 m above the ground. Dots that leave the grid stop.

What it leaves out: clouds, rain, mountains, height, and anything the seeding did. A dot is not a cloud or a pollutant. It is a way of seeing where the air went. Passing near a town is a calculated distance, not exposure or harm.

3. Situate

Popeye tied together a decision in Washington, a test range in California, an air base in Thailand, the roads of Laos, and towns in North Vietnam. The memo itself lists who could be hurt.1

    Off the map: Washington, where the State and Defense departments decided; China Lake, California, where the Navy tested the seeding equipment1; Geneva, where ENMOD opened for signature in 1977.4

    Popeye was one small part of a far larger air war over Laos. The bombs from that war still lie in the ground. In 2018 the UN counted more than 60,000 hectares of confirmed hazardous areas, found since 2015, that hold back farming, roads and schools.5 That is the legacy of bombing, not of seeding. The two are kept apart here.

    4. Narrate

    InterpretiveIn the first week of October 1966, the air over the Se Kong valley drifts southwest. It passes Attapu and Pakxe on the first day, crosses into Cambodia by the third, and reaches the Thai border by the fifth. The rains are ending. In secret, and without asking Laos, a government tries to keep them going, to turn a season into a weapon against trucks. Its own memo cannot say how much rain it would make or where that rain would fall. It names the farmers who might be flooded, then proposes going ahead. Rain does not stay over a road or inside a border. That is the problem the 1977 treaty tried to put into law.
