Two asks at Daegwallyeong

Snow-making by cloud seeding at Daegwallyeong, in Korea’s Taebaek Mountains, 2006–2010, read beside the mountain-god rites held at the same pass.

23 · Audrey Doh · started 2026-11-02 · research in progress

Event

Working question: how did the Daegwallyeong seeding experiments decide when to seed and whether it worked, and how does that compare with the way the rites at the same pass read the sky?

In 2006 and from 2008 to 2010, the Korea Meteorological Administration’s research institute ran cloud seeding experiments to increase snowfall in the Taebaek Mountains. The 2006 ground experiment was at Daegwallyeong, a pass above Pyeongchang and Gangneung. According to the Geoengineering Map, there were nine ground-based experiments in early 2006 and 20 from aircraft over the next three winters. They used silver iodide, calcium chloride, aircraft and flare generators.

Daegwallyeong is also where the Gangneung Danoje festival begins, with rites to the mountain god and the guardian deity of the pass. Both seeding and ritual ask the sky for something, and both face the same hard question: did it happen because of what was done, or would it have happened anyway?

Interpretive: this comparison is the sketch’s framing. It makes no claim that either the seeding or the rites worked.

Simulate

Where could the seeding material have gone? This map releases particles from around the pass and lets the 2006 wind carry them. Pick a day, then watch one day of wind play in about 36 seconds.

Map of Daegwallyeong with 2006 wind and modelled particles. It needs JavaScript and a local server.

Candidate days: 18, 19 and 31 January 2006 are days whose reanalysed weather matches the conditions described for the experiments (easterly wind, humidity of 80 % or more, below freezing). They are not the confirmed experiment days, which are in the paywalled paper.

Reanalysed: the arrows are hourly 10 m wind from ERA5. ERA5 has one value per cell about 25 km across (dashed lines), so Daegwallyeong and Gangneung share a cell. The arrows between cell centres are interpolated.

Modelled: particles start inside the dashed circle, 3 km around the pass, because the generator site is not public. Each moves with the interpolated wind plus a random spread of up to 60 m per step, and is followed for 3 hours. They do not rise, fall, freeze or follow the terrain. They show where the wind pointed, not where silver iodide went or where snow fell.

Cartographic: contours every 200 m from the Copernicus DEM; the thick line is the coast. Places from OpenStreetMap.

Interpretive: the open diamond marks Guksaseonghwangsa and the mountain-god shrine, where the Gangneung Danoje rites begin. Placing it on the same map as the wind is this sketch’s framing, not a claim of any physical link.

Situate

Places and institutions to research. Only the map entry and the first papers support these so far:

  • Daegwallyeong, where the 2006 ground experiment took place and later flights aimed;
  • the rites to the mountain god and guardian deity at Daegwallyeong, which open the Gangneung Danoje festival;
  • the Korea Meteorological Administration and its research institute;
  • the weather radar and satellites that may have recorded the seeding days.

The map above places them. The ERA5 cell on the map is wider than the whole stretch from the pass to the coast: any effect of the seeding would sit inside one reanalysis cell.

Narrate

This is a working page, not a finished case study. The next step is to read the sources behind the map entry and to find what radar, satellites and ritual records can each show about the same mountains.

What this sketch cannot show: where silver iodide actually went, how much snow fell at the pass on any day, whether the seeding or the rites worked, or impacts on people or ecosystems. The wind is a 25 km average, so it misses the way air is forced up and around the pass, which is what the experiments relied on.
